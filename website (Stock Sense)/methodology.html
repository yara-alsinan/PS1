Data, methodology
& reflection.

How Stock Sense was built, what its charts mean, and why I wanted to make a more approachable way to study the market.

01

THE DATA

What is in the project?

Stock Sense uses daily historical price and trading-volume data for 33 recognizable S&P 500 companies. The companies represent all 11 S&P 500 sectors, with three companies selected from each sector. The site covers January 2021 through September 2026.

The original source is S&P 500 Stocks: 25 Years of Data, Updated Daily on Kaggle. The project used its date, open, high, low, close, volume, and ticker fields. Company name, sector, industry, headquarters, and founding year were joined to each price record from the accompanying company file.

02

THE METHOD

How the measures were calculated

Price change

One-day, five-day, one-month, and one-year returns compare a closing price with the closing price 1, 5, 21, or 252 trading days earlier. This makes it easier to compare movement across companies with very different share prices.

52-week drawdown

This is the percentage distance between the latest closing price and the highest closing price in the prior 252 trading days. It shows how far a stock currently sits below its recent high.

Dip Watch

A historical observation is flagged when the closing price declined 5% or more in one day and volume was at least 1.25 times the stock’s prior 30-day average volume. It is a cue to investigate, not a signal to purchase.

Data were processed into a smaller website dataset so the project is easier to load, inspect, and explain. The submitted data includes the cleaned daily records and a separate company/sector lookup file.

03 / REFLECTION

I wanted a way
to start learning.

I have always wanted something simple that would let me study and understand stocks without feeling like I needed to already know everything. I wanted to be more informed for future buying decisions, and this project gave me a way to turn that interest into something visual and easier to explore.

Diversification is also a concept I find smart and interesting. That is why I did not want to build a site around only one famous company or one industry. The data looks across companies in different sectors, from technology and health care to energy, financials, and utilities. It made the comparisons feel more realistic and showed me that companies do not all move for the same reasons or at the same pace.

We have all heard the phrase “buy the dip.” I wanted to see what a dip actually looks like in data instead of treating it like automatic advice. The Dip Watch list lets me look back at recent large declines with unusual trading volume and ask what was happening around them. If a similar move happens in the future, I hope I will be more prepared to research it: what changed, whether the business itself changed, and whether the company fits a diversified portfolio. The site cannot decide what is worth investing in for me, but it can help me slow down and ask better questions.

04

LIMITS

What the data supports—and what it cannot prove

The data supports clear descriptions of historical price movement, trading volume, one-year returns, sector differences, and selected large down days. It can help identify patterns worth studying and make comparisons more understandable.

It cannot predict future stock performance or prove that a price decline is a good buying opportunity. A dip can be caused by new information about a company, a broader market move, earnings, policy changes, or many factors not shown in this dataset. This project also does not include each person’s goals, financial situation, time horizon, taxes, risk tolerance, or the news context behind every change. For those reasons, Stock Sense is an educational tool, not financial advice.
